Lecture 13: Text Features, Bag of Words & TF-IDF
Text Preprocessing & Canonicalization
  • Machine learning models require numeric matrices; raw strings must be mapped to quantitative feature vectors while preserving semantic meaning.
  • Lowercasing (s.str.lower()) ensures casing does not split identical words (e.g. "Chief" and "chief").
  • Punctuation removal (r'[^\w\s]') strips trailing commas, periods, and hyphens so abbreviations map to base tokens.
  • Stop words ("and", "of", "the", "in", "to") are common grammatical words that appear everywhere; removing them prevents counts from being dominated by non-informative terms.
  • Domain cleaning removes Roman numerals (r'\b(i|ii|iii|iv|v)\b') or job codes; privacy ethics requires redacting PII in public salary data.
Bag of Words (BoW) & Counts Matrix
  • Represents each document as an unordered collection of word frequencies from a fixed shared vocabulary $V$.
  • Constructs matrix $M \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times |V|}$ where $M_{i,j} = \text{count}(w_j \text{ in } d_i)$. Grammar, word order, and negation are lost ("not bad, great" produces the exact same vector as "not great, bad").
  • Matrices are >99% zeros; stored as SciPy sparse matrices (csr_matrix).
Similarity: Dot Product vs. Cosine Similarity
  • The raw dot product $\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} = \sum u_j v_j$ scales with document length; two long unrelated essays can have a higher dot product than two short identical summaries.
  • Cosine similarity measures the angle between vectors, normalizing for length:
$$\text{CosineSim}(\vec{u}, \vec{v}) = \cos(\theta) = \frac{\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v}}{\|\vec{u}\|_2 \|\vec{v}\|_2} = \frac{\sum u_j v_j}{\sqrt{\sum u_j^2}\sqrt{\sum v_j^2}}$$
  • Ranges from $0$ (no shared words, orthogonal) to $1$ (identical word proportions). Length-invariant: a short summary and long book on the same topic achieve $\approx 1$.
TF-IDF (Term Frequency – Inverse Document Frequency)
  • Measures how uniquely characteristic word $t$ is to document $d$ within $N$ documents:
$$\text{tfidf}(t, d) = \text{tf}(t, d) \times \text{idf}(t) = \frac{\text{count}(t \text{ in } d)}{\text{total words in } d} \times \log\left(\frac{N}{\text{df}(t)}\right)$$
  • $\text{tf}(t, d)$ = local importance in $d$; $\text{idf}(t)$ = global rarity across all $N$ documents ($\text{df}(t)$ = docs containing $t$).
  • Why $\log$ in IDF: (1) Dampens extreme ratios ($N=10^6, \text{df}=1 \implies 10^6$ dampened to $\approx 13.8$ so IDF does not overpower TF); (2) Captures diminishing returns of rarity (2/50 vs 2/500 docs); (3) Cancels universal words ($\text{df}=N \implies \log(1)=0 \implies \text{tfidf}=0$).
  • TF-IDF is 0 if $t$ is not in $d$ ($\text{tf}=0$) OR if $t$ appears in every single document ($\text{idf}=0$).
  • Presidential Addresses example: common rhetoric ("america", "government") gets TF-IDF $\approx 0$, while distinctive terms stand out (Lincoln: "slaves", FDR: "action").
How to Explain to the Professor
  • Why Cosine Similarity over Euclidean? Euclidean distance grows with text length even for identical topics; Cosine similarity isolates vector direction (word distribution) from magnitude.
  • Out-of-vocabulary words: Ignored during .transform() because vocabulary matrix dimensions are fixed during .fit().
Lecture 14: Linear Regression & Model Evaluation
Modeling Goals & Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM)
  • Models serve two goals: prediction ($\hat{y}_{\text{new}} = H(\vec{x}_{\text{new}})$) and inference (interpreting weights $w_j$).
  • Residual $e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i = y_i - H(x_i)$ is signed observation error. Squared loss $L=(y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2$ penalizes large deviations quadratically.
  • Mean Squared Error (MSE / Empirical Risk) & Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE):
$$R_{\text{sq}}(H) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - H(x_i))^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n e_i^2 \quad \Big| \quad \text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\text{MSE}}$$
  • RMSE is expressed in the exact same physical units as response $y$ (e.g. dollars).
Constant Baseline & Simple Linear Regression (SLR)
  • Optimal constant model $H(x)=c$ minimizing squared error is sample mean $c^* = \bar{y}$.
  • RMSE of constant model is standard deviation $\sigma_y = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum(y_i - \bar{y})^2}$. A regression model is only useful if its $\text{RMSE} < \sigma_y$.
  • Simple Linear Regression $H(x) = w_0 + w_1 x$:
$$w_1^* = r \frac{\sigma_y}{\sigma_x} = \frac{\text{Cov}(x, y)}{\sigma_x^2} = \frac{\sum(x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum(x_i - \bar{x})^2} \quad \Big| \quad w_0^* = \bar{y} - w_1^* \bar{x}$$
  • 3 Key SLR Properties: (1) Line passes through centroid $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$; (2) Average residual is zero ($\sum e_i = 0$); (3) Residuals are uncorrelated with input $x$ ($\sum x_i e_i = 0$).
Multiple Linear Regression & Normal Equation
  • Model with $d$ features: $H(\vec{x}) = w_0 + w_1 x^{(1)} + \dots + w_d x^{(d)} = \vec{x}^T \vec{w}$.
  • Design matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times (d+1)}$ has leading column of 1s for intercept $w_0$.
  • Predictions: $\hat{\vec{y}} = X\vec{w}$; Residuals: $\vec{e} = \vec{y} - X\vec{w}$. Normal Equation gives optimal weights:
$$\vec{w}^* = (X^T X)^{-1} X^T \vec{y}$$
  • $X^T X$ is invertible iff $X$ has full column rank $d+1$ (no collinear features and $n > d$).
Model Assessment: R-Squared ($R^2$) & Residual Plots
  • Coefficient of Determination ($R^2$) = fraction of variance in $y$ explained by model:
$$R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum(y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{\sum(y_i - \bar{y})^2} = 1 - \frac{\text{MSE}(H)}{\text{MSE}(\text{mean model})} \quad \Big| \quad \text{RMSE} = \sigma_y \sqrt{1 - R^2}$$
  • $R^2 = 1 \implies \text{RMSE}=0$; $R^2 = 0 \implies$ same as predicting mean $\bar{y}$; $R^2 < 0 \implies$ worse than mean (happens on test data). In SLR with intercept, $R^2 = r^2$.
  • Residual Plots: Homoscedastic random scatter around 0 = good linear fit; U-shape/curvature = non-linear relationship; Funnel = heteroscedasticity.
How to Explain to the Professor
  • Adding features effect: Training RMSE always decreases or stays the same ($R^2$ increases), but test RMSE can increase due to overfitting.
  • Why can test $R^2$ be negative? If test MSE exceeds test variance $\text{Var}(y_{\text{test}})$, then $1 - \frac{\text{MSE}}{\text{Var}} < 0$.

Lecture 15: Feature Engineering & Transformers
The Role of Feature Engineering
  • Linear regression is restricted to flat hyperplanes $\vec{w}^T \vec{x}$ directly on input features; real relationships are non-linear, categorical, and multi-scale.
  • Feature engineering transforms raw domain columns $X$ into engineered numerical features $\phi(X)$, allowing linear models to capture curves and categories while keeping optimization linear in weights $\vec{w}$. Drop uninformative features (IDs, constants).
Categorical Encoding: Ordinal vs. Nominal
  • Ordinal Features (Ordered): Education (HS < BS < MS < PhD), Ratings (Poor < Fair < Good < Great). Map directly to integers preserving order: df['rating'].map({'Poor':1, 'Fair':2, 'Good':3, 'Great':4}).
  • Nominal Features (Unordered): Smoker (Yes/No), Day (Thur, Fri, Sat, Sun). Arbitrary integers (Thur=0, Fri=1, Sat=2, Sun=3) falsely assume Sun > Thur and equal distances.
  • One-Hot Encoding (OHE): Creates binary $0/1$ indicator column $\mathbb{I}(\text{col}=c)$ for each category.
  • Geometry of OHE: Model $\hat{\text{tip}} = w_0 + w_1 \text{bill} + w_2 \text{size} + w_3 \mathbb{I}(\text{smoker}=\text{Yes})$:
    • Non-Smoker: $\hat{\text{tip}} = w_0 + w_1 \text{bill} + w_2 \text{size}$
    • Smoker: $\hat{\text{tip}} = (w_0 + w_3) + w_1 \text{bill} + w_2 \text{size}$
    • Creates two parallel hyperplanes with identical slopes separated by intercept shift $w_3$.
Linearization & Quantitative Scaling
  • Linearization (Horsepower vs. MPG): Raw scatter shows curved decay; linear fit gives high error. Transforming feature to $\log(\text{hp})$ or $1/\text{hp}$ linearizes relationship: $$\hat{\text{mpg}} = w_0 + w_1 \log(\text{hp})$$ Flattens curved residuals to random scatter, lowers RMSE, raises $R^2$.
  • StandardScaler() standardizes via $z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}$ (mean 0, variance 1). MinMaxScaler() rescales to $[0, 1]$. Binarizer(threshold=t) maps to binary $\mathbb{I}(x > t)$.
  • Scaling Nuance: In standard OLS linear regression, standardizing features does not change predictions, residuals, or $R^2$; it only rescales weights $w_j$. (Scaling is essential for Ridge/Lasso, PCA, and KNN).
Scikit-Learn Transformer Architecture & Data Leakage
  • Transformers (Preprocessors): Implement .fit(), .transform(), .fit_transform().
  • Estimators (Models): Implement .fit(), .predict(), .score().
  • .fit(X) learns parameters ($\mu, \sigma$ in StandardScaler; categories in OneHotEncoder); .transform(X) applies transformation using stored parameters.
  • Golden Rule: Never fit a transformer on test data. Always fit on $X_{\text{train}}$, then transform both $X_{\text{train}}$ and $X_{\text{test}}$ to prevent data leakage.
How to Explain to the Professor
  • Why drop='first' in OneHotEncoder? Prevents the Dummy Variable Trap. Keeping all $K$ dummy columns alongside an intercept creates linear dependence ($\sum \text{dummies} = 1 = \text{intercept}$), making $X^T X$ singular and non-invertible.
  • Transformer vs. Estimator: Transformers preprocess feature matrices ($X \to X'$) via transform; Estimators learn predictive mappings ($X \to y$) via predict.
Lecture 16: Pipelines, Multicollinearity & Generalization
Pipelines & ColumnTransformers
  • ColumnTransformer applies specific transformers to specified column subsets (e.g. scaling numerics, one-hot encoding categoricals) and concatenates results.
  • FunctionTransformer(func) wraps custom Python functions (e.g. np.log) into an sklearn transformer.
  • Pipeline chains sequential transformers with a final model estimator. Calling pl.fit(X_tr, y_tr) fits all steps in order; pl.predict(X_te) transforms test data using training parameters and predicts, eliminating data leakage.
Multicollinearity & The Dummy Variable Trap
  • Multicollinearity occurs when one feature is a linear combination of other features.
  • Height Example (Inches $x_1$ vs. CM $x_2 = 2.54 x_1$): $$\hat{y} = w_0 + w_1 x_1 + w_2 (2.54 x_1) = w_0 + (w_1 + 2.54 w_2) x_1$$ Any pair where $w_1 + 2.54 w_2 = 3$ gives identical predictions and minimal error ($w_1 = 1000, w_2 = -392.5$). Columns of $X$ are linearly dependent $\implies \det(X^T X)=0 \implies (X^T X)^{-1}$ does not exist.
  • Crucial Distinction: Multicollinearity does not hurt prediction accuracy or generalization on similar data; it completely destroys interpretability because coefficients become unstable and arbitrarily large.
Generalization & Bias-Variance Decomposition
  • Generalization evaluates model performance on unseen population data.
  • Underfitting (High Bias): Model too simple (e.g. line for cubic data); high train RMSE, high test RMSE.
  • Overfitting (High Variance): Model overly complex (e.g. degree 25 poly); near-zero train RMSE, massive test RMSE (memorizes sample noise).
  • The Bias-Variance Decomposition of Generalization Risk:
$$\mathbb{E}[(y_{\text{new}} - H(x_{\text{new}}))^2] = \text{Model Bias}^2 + \text{Model Variance} + \text{Observation Error } \sigma^2$$
  • Model Bias $(\mathbb{E}[H(x)] - f(x))^2$: Error from simplistic assumptions.
  • Model Variance $\mathbb{E}[(H(x) - \mathbb{E}[H(x)])^2] \propto \frac{d}{n}$: Prediction sensitivity to training sample.
  • Observation Error $\sigma^2$: Irreducible natural random measurement noise.
  • Sample size $n \uparrow \implies$ variance $\to 0$ (bias unchanged). Feature count $d \uparrow \implies$ bias $\downarrow$, variance $\uparrow$.
Train-Test Splits & Validation Diagnostics
  • Training error reflects only bias; test error reflects both bias and variance.
  • $\text{RMSE}_{\text{train}} \approx \text{RMSE}_{\text{test}}$ (both low) $\implies$ well-calibrated, generalizes well.
  • $\text{RMSE}_{\text{train}} \ll \text{RMSE}_{\text{test}} \implies$ overfitting (high variance); collect more $n$, regularize, drop features.
  • $\text{RMSE}_{\text{train}} \approx \text{RMSE}_{\text{test}}$ (both high) $\implies$ underfitting (high bias); feature engineering, non-linear terms.
How to Explain to the Professor
  • Why degree 25 polynomial fails on test data: Zero bias on training sample, but massive variance; interpolates sample noise rather than population signal.
  • Variance formula intuition: In linear regression, model variance is proportional to $\frac{d}{n}$ ($d$ = features, $n$ = observations).
